The First Thing I Built With AI

October 4, 2026

It was last November, 2025, when I first started building with AI. I can't remember what led to it.

As a trader, when I hear about a trend, it's usually too late. I feel like I am the signal.

Time and time again, I have been that silly little broke retail trader who hears about a hot company and gets all excited. Usually that means you're late to the party. As soon as you get in, join the party (by buying the stock or taking a position in the stock), then it crashes, proving to me once again, I have no idea.

No idea is original. If you have it, someone else most likely does too.

Yes, in trading, I am my own tell. I have to be fully aware of how overexcited I am, remembering that if this info came to me, it must have also just reached a ton of other retail traders out there, most of which probably have more experience and capital than I do.

But an old trading mentor taught me that we are not trying to beat every other trader out there. Just like in the gym, there will always be someone bigger and badder than you. Same in trading.

No, he said, we aren't trying to beat the hedge fund managers, though many retail traders do. No, we are just looking to participate. Be involved. Be aware of your surroundings and don't let opportunity pass you by.

Don't miss the rally.

We are not always going to beat the market, but we definitely don't want to miss it.

I guess this is a long-winded intro into how I feel about AI.

I don't want to miss it.

I want to participate.

And the pessimistic part of the trader comes out.

Whoa, here's this cool thing. I need to learn how this works.

Oh, but what's the point? Someone else will be better at it. Someone else will do it first.

But who?

I don't know any AI people.

Gemini was my first love. I became obsessed.

Of course, I used AI to research stocks. As a close passion of mine is income investing, I felt it still is a very misunderstood and understudied part of retail investing.

I was lucky enough to be part of an online community of like-minded investors, of traders, that shared my passion. A buddy of mine had a master list that I thought was so cool. It had every income ETF available on it there, and he updated it every week or month with the distributions, and every week we'd check his Google Sheet to see who paid the highest dividend that week.

We were technical analyst students and thought we understood NAV erosion because we could read a chart. Technical analysis gives us an edge over the competition, always.

So we thought we would track distributions, NAV erosion, price movement, and stock strength relative to its peers and to the market.

In 2023, we rode YieldMax's famed funds to all-time highs, thinking the new product type was the future of investing.

The concept was somewhat simple: invest into an income fund, a good one, and once the income you receive paid back the original investment, everything else is passive income, free cash flow from then on.

So I became obsessed with trying to find funds that beat the underlying.

And this led to my first use case building with AI.

Could I just see the damn thing?

There will always be debate as to the effectiveness or longevity of these strategies. Traditional investors will say they are a scam. I have argued with middle-aged financial guys about just this: dividends are unsustainable, etc.

Like many strategies, it is how you look at it.

What is your time horizon?

What are your goals with this capital?

There are many factors that go into income investing. They serve a very specific need for a very specific investor.

What they say is not about the metrics, the merits of income investing. It is a story of my first AI build.

Finding option income funds that beat the underlying was the goal, and I would use AI to help visualize it.

And little did I know this would lead to a passion of digital creativity that I never knew I could access.

The spreadsheet was useful, but I wanted to actually see what these investments were doing.

If I put $10,000 into an option-income ETF, I wanted three lines.

Show me the underlying stock.

Show me the income fund itself.

And then show me the actual journey of the investor who owned the income fund and collected all of those distributions along the way.

Because looking at the price chart alone can make some of these funds look absolutely horrific.

The share price can erode and erode and erode while cash is being removed from the investment and deposited into your account. Ignore the cash and you aren't looking at the same investment anymore.

That distinction eventually became what we call cumulative value on the Retail Investor Report.

But back then I didn't have a name for it.

I just wanted the computer to draw me the picture.

So I asked Gemini.

I think the first version was Python running through Streamlit. I didn't know Python. I didn't know Streamlit. I didn't really know what any of the infrastructure underneath the thing was doing.

Gemini would tell me to make an account somewhere.

Okay.

Click.

Make another account.

Okay.

Click.

Paste this here.

Sure.

And eventually the thing rendered.

And there it fucking was.

The chart.

This is probably the moment the whole thing changed for me.

Because it wasn't an idea anymore.

I had asked a computer to take something that existed in my head — something I had wanted to be able to visualize as an investor — and it made it.

And it was beautiful.

You could see the fund deteriorating. You could see the distributions coming out. You could see the underlying. And you could finally see whether all of that cash actually compensated you for owning the income vehicle instead of simply owning the stock underneath it.

Suddenly I didn't want to do it for one ticker.

I wanted to do it for every ticker.

Okay. Now build the whole thing.

That created the next obvious problem.

If this chart works for one income ETF, why couldn't I type in any ticker and get the same answer?

And if we were going to do that, why couldn't the master spreadsheet become a database?

The issuers were already announcing their distributions. They published ex-dividend dates, pay dates and distribution amounts. Some of them emailed the announcements directly to investors.

Why were we manually hunting all of this down every week and typing it into a Google Sheet?

Could the machine do that too?

That question eventually became the Income Board.

The idea was to have every income ETF in one place with its latest distribution, ex-dividend date, pay date, yield, annualized yield and strategy information. Then you could click into a fund and stop looking at the flashy headline yield for a second.

You could see what actually happened.

What did the fund pay you?

What happened to your principal?

What did the underlying do during the same period?

And most importantly: when you put the cash and the remaining asset together, did the thing actually work?

That original visualization eventually grew into the Performance Backtester I use today when I write the Retail Investor Report.

The funny thing is that I didn't start out trying to build a financial website.

I was trying to answer a question.

Then something else clicked.

Once I started, I couldn't stop.

I had just asked for this. It's something I always wanted to visualize, and I just asked for it, and I didn't just get it.

I had to set up a bunch of stuff first.

But I just clicked buttons.

I didn't have to understand it.

I didn't have to write code.

I just asked for it.

My mind was blown.

I'm a trained designer. I can think up a solution, but the journey to realization has always been so exhausting.

I studied product design. I learned things like SolidWorks and Photoshop. Later I gravitated toward physical things. Wood. Metal. Furniture. Houses. I built an RV trailer. I liked making things I could actually touch.

There was always this enormous distance between imagining something and making the damn thing exist.

Materials. Tools. Skills. Money. Time.

And software was worse.

I could imagine software. I could describe what I wanted software to do. But that didn't mean I could make software.

AI changed that relationship almost overnight.

Now we live in a world where, as an artist, I can have anything I can articulate.

I have been given the powers of those that run the simulation.

The world can be given to those who describe it and visualize it.

I wonder how other artists feel sometimes. Other creatives like me who feel like they have been given a free credit card to the art and supply store.

Maybe they might feel a lot different to those that are established in their field, making money off their craft. Their fear that their customers might not need them anymore is understandable.

But I feel lucky.

I feel empowered and blessed.

Because the Income Board wasn't really the important thing I built.

It was just the first thing.

What mattered was realizing that there was suddenly a bridge between the things I could imagine and the things I could make.

I didn't become a programmer that day.

I became a builder again.

And once I realized that, I couldn't stop building.
