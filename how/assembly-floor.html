HOW IT WORKS · 02

Assembly Floor OS

A company operating system for manufacturers — written by the person who was running the production floor it was written for. Not a software company's guess at the work. The work, then the software.

BUILT INSIDE A WASTEWATER INSTRUMENTATION MANUFACTURER · IN PRODUCTION USE

01 · THE PROBLEM

The floor and the office disagree.

A production operation runs on more systems than anyone admits: paper travellers, a spreadsheet somebody maintains, a whiteboard that is authoritative until it is wiped, and a great deal of one person's memory. None of them are wrong individually. The problem is that they never agree at the same moment.

So the office quotes a date the floor cannot hit. Someone reorders a part that is already on a shelf. A device in the field is listed as healthy because the last person to touch it has not written it up yet. Every one of those is a coordination failure, not an effort failure — and you cannot fix coordination failures by working harder, which is exactly what most floors try first.

02 · WHY THIS ONE IS DIFFERENT

I was the operator, not the vendor.

I did not interview operators to gather requirements. I was second in command of the operation — running production day to day, the people, the throughput, the cost, the quality, and the ordering — and I built this alongside doing that job.

That changes what gets built. Software written by someone who has never had to answer for a missed ship date optimises for what demos well. Software written by the person who will be answering for it optimises for the two minutes before a decision gets made. The features that survived are the ones I needed on a bad Tuesday, and the ones that did not survive are the ones that looked good in a plan and cost time on the floor.

03 · THE CORE MOVE

One record. Everybody reads it.

Jobs, sites and field devices go into one record, and both the floor and the office read that record rather than their own copy of it. This is unglamorous and it is the entire product. Nearly every operational argument I have ever had traces back to two people holding different numbers and each being certain.

It matters that this is a source-of-truth design and not a reporting layer bolted on top. A dashboard that reads from four systems inherits the disagreement it was supposed to resolve; it just renders the disagreement more attractively.

04 · THE PAPERWORK

It eats what a factory actually produces.

Every operation generates a steady volume of documents that matter and that nobody has time to key in: purchase orders, packing lists, service reports, field notes, photographs of a nameplate taken because it was faster than writing the serial down. The usual outcome is that this material is technically retained and practically unfindable.

So intake accepts the mess as it arrives, reads it — including scans with no text layer, which is most of what a real operation produces — and attaches it to the job, site or device it belongs to. The test is not whether it can parse a clean document. The test is whether a photograph of a smudged label taken in a plant room ends up on the right device record.

05 · THE ASSISTANT

Pointed at the operation's own data.

There is an AI assistant, and the only interesting thing about it is what it reads. It is wired into the same record the floor runs on — not a separate index, not a copy synced on a schedule, not a general model told about your business in a prompt.

That distinction is the difference between an assistant that can answer "which devices at this site are overdue and what did we last ship there" and one that produces a confident paragraph about manufacturing in general. If the assistant's data can drift from the operational data, it will, and the first time it does the floor stops trusting it permanently. Assistants do not get a second chance at credibility.

06 · HONESTLY

What it is not.

It would be easy to describe this as a full ERP replacement. It is not, and claiming so to an operations audience is how you lose them in the first meeting:

  • It is not an ERP or an accounting system. It does not run your general ledger, your payroll, or your statutory reporting, and it is not trying to.
  • It does not schedule the floor for you. It makes the state of the floor visible and shared. The judgement about what to run next is still a human one, and I would be suspicious of any product that claimed otherwise.
  • An empty tenant is still a real setup job. Bootstrapping a new operation from nothing is the open piece of product work, and pretending onboarding is trivial is the most common lie in this category.
  • Custom scope is a change order, not a feature. Deliberately. An operations tool that bends to every request becomes an unmaintainable fork per customer, and then nobody gets fixes.

The origin is anonymised here on purpose. The system runs inside a live manufacturer, and the specifics of that business are theirs, not mine to publish.
